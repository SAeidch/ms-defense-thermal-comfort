🏫
People spend most of their time indoors (Lowther et al., 2019). In schools and universities, the indoor environment affects health, productivity and learning (Sun et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2023). A reliable comfort signal is what occupant-centered HVAC control needs as its input.
Three numbers frame the problem. First, fewer than 20 percent of buildings meet the target of 80 percent thermally satisfied occupants. Second, personalized conditioning has been shown in simulations to save up to about 60 percent of HVAC energy, so there is a strong incentive to know how occupants feel. Third, the standard model, PMV, needs six inputs, some of which are hard to measure continuously. A comfort signal that is reliable and cheap to obtain is the missing input for occupant-centered control.

The catch: personalized models usually need many sensors, some of them intrusive, and many labeled comfort votes in every new building. That burden makes deployment harder (Aerts, 2019; Čulić et al., 2021).
PMV uses four environmental and two personal inputs and predicts the average vote of a large group, so it misses individual differences. Wu and colleagues found only about 30 percent accuracy for PMV under cooling and cold exposure. Personalized machine learning models do better, often by adding physiological signals. But they create a deployment problem: many sensors, some intrusive, and many labeled votes in every new building. That is the gap this work addresses.
Introduction

The research question

Can thermal comfort be predicted with acceptable accuracy from a reduced set of practical sensors and only a small amount of locally labeled data?
CONSTRAINT 1 · DIMENSIONALITY

Fewer variables

Air temperature, relative humidity and a smartwatch heart rate instead of a full environmental, wearable and camera setup.

CONSTRAINT 2 · DATA

Fewer labels

Hundreds of comfort votes from one building instead of tens of thousands.

Strategy: transfer learning. Learn general comfort patterns from a large public database (ASHRAE Global Thermal Comfort Database II, 44,124 observations), then adapt them to the small, low-dimensional local data with a lightweight adapter.
This is the research question. The final aim was to see whether we can use fewer variables and still reach acceptable accuracy. There are two constraints: fewer variables, meaning low input dimensionality, and fewer labels, meaning little local data. The strategy is transfer learning: pretrain on the ASHRAE Global Thermal Comfort Database II, with 44,124 observations after preprocessing, and adapt the learned representation to our small local dataset through an adapter.

Open question: can a model pretrained on the large ASHRAE DB II be adapted to a reduced wearable and environmental sensor set with little local data, and does the backbone, Transformer or recurrent, matter for that transfer?
Recurrent networks such as LSTMs are the common choice for comfort prediction. Physiological models often rely on several body-worn sensors. Transformers have started to appear: Zhang and Li used transfer learning with a Transformer pretrained on RP-884 with five sensation classes, and ComfortGPT predicts thermostat setpoints rather than sensation. Our own chamber study used an LSTM with full sensing. What remained open is whether a model pretrained on DB II can be adapted to a reduced sensor set with little local data, and whether the backbone matters for that transfer.

The steps are ordered by logic, not by calendar: the field campaign ran first, and its direct-training results (Indoor Air 2024) motivated the transfer-learning design.

The work has two experiments, which I present as two steps. Step one is a full-sensing chamber experiment: environmental sensors, an Empatica E4 wristband and a thermal camera. It asks which signals actually track thermal sensation when we measure almost everything. Step two is the main work: an occupied office and classroom where we measured only air temperature, humidity and a smartwatch heart rate. Because that data is low-dimensional and small, we pretrained on the ASHRAE database and added our data through an adapter. In calendar terms, the field campaign came first; the order here follows the logic of reducing variables.
Research design

Contributions

01 · DATA

A reduced-sensing field dataset

596 synchronized observations from 15 occupants in two occupied rooms: air temperature, relative humidity, wrist heart rate and three-class thermal sensation labels.

02 · SOURCE MODEL

A Transformer source model on ASHRAE DB II

T3C (developed with H. Aminorroayaei; ASHRAE 2026), trained and validated on 44,124 observations (80/20 split) converted into one-hour pseudo-temporal sequences of 17 features.

03 · TRANSFER

Adapter-based transfer learning

A small adapter maps six local features into the pretrained T3C, with encoder layers 1–5 frozen; a BiLSTM adapted analogously (adapter, retrained layer 2, classifier) is the comparison.

Evaluation under class imbalance

Accuracy, macro-F1 and per-class recall over repeated seeds, with attention to the rare warm-discomfort class.

There are four contributions. First, a reduced-sensing field dataset with 596 synchronized observations from 15 occupants. Second, the T3C Transformer pretrained on ASHRAE DB II after converting static records into sequences. Third, the adapter-based transfer, compared against a BiLSTM backbone. Fourth, an evaluation that reports macro-F1 and per-class recall, not only accuracy, because the warm-discomfort class is rare.

The campaign produced 596 synchronized observations. Each is a 12-step, five-minute sequence from a session, paired with a thermal sensation vote. Warm discomfort is rare, under five percent of observations, which matters for the results. Comfort scores were almost identical in the two rooms, but heart rate was significantly higher in Room 1, about 83 versus 75 beats per minute. So heart rate alone is not a universal comfort meter; it carries information together with the environment. In Room 1, cool votes came at lower temperatures, about 21.9 versus 22.5 degrees for neutral. Why it is hard

  • Few labels: hundreds of votes, not tens of thousands
  • Few inputs: a handful of sensed signals per time step
  • Imbalance: warm discomfort is rare in conditioned rooms (Quintana et al., 2020)
  • Subjective, noisy labels from occupant votes

Before transfer learning, we trained models from scratch on this same field data, which we presented at Indoor Air 2024. The RNN-LSTM beat a feed-forward ANN, but accuracy on the seven-point vote was only above 50 percent. These are seven-point numbers, so they are not directly comparable to the three-class results later. The problem is structural: few labels, few inputs, a rare warm class and noisy subjective votes. That motivated the idea of borrowing knowledge from the large ASHRAE database.

Each record becomes the final step t of a 12-step sequence; the 11 earlier steps add zero-mean Gaussian jitter to the five transient variables and hold the rest constant. Values and jitter size shown here are illustrative. The history is synthesized, not measured. Press → to step through.
ASHRAE records are snapshots: one set of conditions at the moment of the survey. Both of our backbones are sequence models, so we built a one-hour history for every record. The actual record becomes the last of 12 five-minute steps. For the 11 earlier steps, we add zero-mean Gaussian jitter to the five transient variables, air temperature, humidity, air velocity, metabolic rate and SET, and keep the static features constant. Then we add first differences of the transient variables, which gives 17 features per step and a tensor of N by 12 by 17. I can regenerate the jitter to show that each history is one random sample. It is important to say that this history is synthesized, not measured, and I return to that in the limitations.

Why last-token pooling

Step 12 is the moment of the survey, the only real observation in an ASHRAE sequence. Through attention it already summarizes the 11 reconstructed steps before it.

Hover a block for details. T3C source model: Aminorroayaei, Chahardoli et al., ASHRAE Annual Conference 2026.

This is the T3C architecture. A linear layer projects the 17 features at each step into 256 dimensions. A learned positional embedding adds order information. Then come six encoder layers, each with eight-head self-attention and a feed-forward network that expands to 512 and returns to 256, with residual connections and layer normalization. We pool the last token, because step 12 is the actual survey moment, and through attention it already summarizes the earlier steps. A linear layer then gives three class probabilities. The model has about 3.2 million parameters, which I estimated from the layer sizes.

Why the connection points differ

Every T3C layer reads and writes 256-d vectors, so the adapter plugs in ahead of the frozen stack. BiLSTM layer 1 is tied to the 17 ASHRAE inputs, so its adapter enters at layer 2 (512-d), which is retrained from its pretrained weights.

Adapter training

Seeds 42, 123, 795. Class balancing on the training partition only. Validation keeps 120 unaltered observations per run (54 Neutral, 60 Cool, 6 Warm).

Trained parameters (T3C adapter)
≈0.56M of ≈3.2 M

This is the core method. For T3C, the adapter is two linear projections, from 6 to 128 and from 128 to 256, the width the encoder expects. It replaces the original 17-to-256 projection. The positional embedding and encoder layers one to five stay frozen with their ASHRAE weights, and only layer six, the classifier and the adapter are trained. For the BiLSTM, layer one is tied to the 17 ASHRAE inputs, so the adapter must project to 512 and enter at layer two; layer two starts from its pretrained weights and is retrained with the classifier, so nothing in the BiLSTM path stays frozen. During adaptation we balanced classes only in the training partition and kept the validation data untouched: 120 real observations per run, of which only six are warm. Roughly 18 percent of the T3C parameters are trained.

Discussion

Why the backbones diverged, and what the evidence does not show
Let me discuss why the two backbones behaved differently, and the limits of the evidence.
Discussion

Same source accuracy, different transferability

01 · INTERFACE

A uniform 256-d interface

Every encoder layer reads and writes the same width, so the adapter supplies inputs in the format the frozen layers already expect. In the BiLSTM, the adapter must replace the input sequence of layer 2, whose weights were tuned to layer-1 outputs.

02 · STABILITY

Lower variance across seeds

1.36 vs 2.39 pp SD in accuracy, and narrower learning-curve bands, which is consistent with a more stable transfer.

03 · SIGNAL

Heart rate may help in context

Three sensed signals were enough for 73% three-class accuracy, consistent with physiological comfort studies (Chen et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2021) and with transfer learning under label scarcity (Somu et al., 2021; Zhang & Li, 2023). No run without heart rate was made, so its contribution is not isolated.

🧪
These are interpretations, not controlled tests. Architecture and model capacity were not separated, and with six warm cases per run the per-class differences are fragile.
Why did the backbones diverge after transfer when they tied on the source? My interpretation has three parts. First, the interface: every Transformer layer uses the same 256-dimensional width, so the adapter can feed the frozen stack inputs in the format it already expects. In the BiLSTM, the adapter has to replace the input of layer two, whose weights were tuned to the outputs of layer one. Second, stability: the Transformer adapter varied less across seeds. Third, the signal: heart rate plus the environment was enough for about 73 percent, consistent with the physiological comfort literature, although we did not run a model without heart rate, so its own contribution is not isolated. I want to be clear that these are interpretations. We did not run controlled experiments that separate architecture from capacity.
Discussion

Limitations

Data

  • Rare warm discomfort: 6 warm cases per validation run, so per-class recall is fragile
  • Small cohort: 15 participants, two rooms, one climate, November–January only, so the season features vary little
  • New occupants: how the 120 validation observations were drawn is not documented, and they may share participants with training; leave-one-participant-out has not been evaluated
  • No separate local test set: the 120 validation observations are the only held-out local data; if they also selected the reported epoch (the mean curves peak below the reported means), the adapter scores are optimistic

Source model

  • Pseudo-temporal sequences: the ASHRAE history is synthesized, not measured
  • Subjective inputs: thermal acceptability and preference are source-model inputs that sensors cannot provide, so source accuracy may be optimistic (the local adapter does not use them)
  • Hold-out reuse: the ASHRAE hold-out also drove the learning-rate schedule, so it is not a fully independent test

Comparison

  • Architecture and trained capacity are confounded: the BiLSTM adapter retrains its whole second layer, T3C only its top layer
  • No from-scratch or classical baseline on the same local split, so the gain from pretraining is not isolated
  • Three seeds per configuration

Step 1 chamber study

  • 5 participants; setpoints rose over the campaign, so IAQ variables are confounded with temperature
I want to be transparent about the limitations. On the data side, the warm class is very small, the cohort is small and covers only one winter season, and we have not tested generalization to new occupants with a leave-one-participant-out design. On the source side, the ASHRAE histories are synthesized, and two of the source inputs, thermal acceptability and preference, are survey answers that a sensor cannot provide. That may make source accuracy optimistic, although the local adapter does not use them. The architecture comparison does not separate architecture from capacity. And the chamber study had five participants, with temperature confounded with session order.
Conclusion

Conclusions

73.33%
Reduced sensing is feasible here. Air temperature, RH and wrist heart rate, plus date-derived context, reached 73.33% ± 1.36% accuracy (macro-F1 0.667) on local three-class comfort.
1.4%
Transfer is feasible with little data. With 596 local observations in total (about 476 for training per run), 1.4% of the source size, the adapted Transformer reached 73%; a baseline without pretraining on the same split has not been run, so the gain from transfer is not yet isolated.
+5.55pp
The backbone may matter for transfer. T3C and the BiLSTM tie on ASHRAE (71.06% vs 71.07%), but the Transformer adapter led after transfer in all three seeds, with higher macro-F1 and lower variance; architecture and trained capacity are not yet separated.
ρ 0.77

To conclude. First, reduced sensing is feasible: three sensed signals plus date-derived context reached 73 percent accuracy on local three-class comfort. Second, transfer is feasible with little data: 596 local observations in total were enough to adapt the model, although a baseline without pretraining is still needed to isolate the gain from transfer. Third, the backbone may matter for transfer: the two backbones tied on the source data, but the Transformer adapter led in all three seeds after transfer. And fourth, the full-sensing chamber study found air temperature to be the dominant signal, which is consistent with the reduced design. Together these support the feasibility of low-burden, occupant-centered comfort prediction in real buildings.
Conclusion

Future work

Stronger evaluation

  • Leave-one-participant-out validation
  • More warm-discomfort samples
  • Source models without subjective inputs

Richer data

  • A full year, so the seasonal encoding covers its whole cycle
  • Wrist skin temperature as an added low-burden signal
  • More rooms and climates

Toward control

  • Step-wise rather than sequence-wise predictions for HVAC feedback
  • Physics priors such as PMV/SET with a learned residual
  • Coupling predictions to action: an LLM reasoning layer grounded in ASHRAE 55 (ongoing)
Future work has three directions. Stronger evaluation: leave-one-participant-out validation, more warm samples, and source models without subjective inputs. Richer data: a full year of collection so the seasonal encoding covers its whole cycle, wrist skin temperature as another low-burden signal, and more rooms and climates. And moving toward control: step-wise predictions for HVAC feedback, physics priors, and connecting predictions to actions, which we are exploring with an LLM reasoning layer grounded in ASHRAE Standard 55.

Short answers to likely questions

Is 73% “acceptable”?

It is at the level the source model reached with 17 features (71%, on a different dataset), on a three-class problem with rare warm cases. Practical acceptability depends on how predictions feed control; step-wise feedback is future work.

Why not PMV directly?

PMV needs mean radiant temperature, air velocity, clothing and metabolic rate, which are hard to measure continuously, and it predicts a group average rather than an individual.

Why freeze layers 1–5?

With 596 observations, updating about 3 M parameters risks overwriting what was learned from 44 k observations. Selective fine-tuning keeps general features and adapts the top (Iman et al., 2023).

Does the synthetic history bias the model?

Possibly. The jitter adds random drift before each survey record, and its physical size in the code is still being re-checked against the manuscript. Measured histories, like the local 5-minute data, are the better source.

What does “personalized” mean here?

Adapted to these occupants and this building with local data, not one model per person. Per-occupant fine-tuning is a natural next step.

How good is 73% against a trivial baseline?

Always predicting the majority class (Uncomfortably Cool) gives 50% on the local validation set; the adapter reaches 73.33% with macro-F1 0.667.
